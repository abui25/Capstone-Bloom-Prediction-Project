Abstract:

Throughout the annals of history, scientists have recorded blossom dates for hundreds of plant species, with written records dating back to 812 A. D. This body of work remains highly pertinent today, as blossom dates shed light on the effects of anthropogenic influence on ecological systems and provide insight into an ever-changing climate. There have been several recent studies that explored strategies to improve blossom date predictions. For instance, Chung, Uran et al. illustrated how a nonlinear model that accounts for dormancy release and forcing stages could perform reasonably well across temporal and spatial variations. Similarly, Toshihiko Sugiura et al. demonstrated that interactions between ecodormancy and endodormancy, studied in a laboratory environment, can be used to develop a model independent of historical data that avoids overfitting. Finally, Yochifumi Masago and Lian Maychee took yet another approach, using machine learning techniques such as gradient boosting decision trees, random forests, and neural networks to analyze bloom predictions. 

While modern approaches can significantly improve upon Quetelet’s original framework for predicting bloom dates, they often become unwieldy and difficult to interpret despite accurately capturing complex dynamics. Quetelet’s initial model, thus, remains popular because it effectively communicates predictions to professionals across disciplines. The purpose of this study is to design a model that maintains the simplicity of historical models while improving their accuracy. Throughout the literature review, one common theme that emerged was the exploration of two key phenological phases not accounted for in Quetelet’s original model: winter chilling and spring warming.  Thus, a multiple linear regression model was designed to account for chilling, warming, and their interaction. The chilling phase was calculated by summing the number of days below 32 degrees Fahrenheit from November through January, inclusive. The warming, or ecodormancy, phase was calculated by accumulating the total heat between February and April – total heat was defined as the excess heat above a baseline of 45 degrees. It was hypothesized that by incorporating both dormancy-related chilling and spring thermal forcing, this modified framework would yield more accurate bloom date predictions, while still preserving the interpretability of Quetelet’s model.

Hold-out validation was used to analyze prediction accuracy. Across the three regions studied — Washington, DC, United States; Kyoto, Japan; and Liestal, Switzerland — the MAE of the linear models improved over the baseline MAE, which was calculated by averaging the bloom date for each year. In DC, the MAE dropped from 5.71 to 3.5 (42% improvement), in Kyoto, the MAE dropped from 5.9 to 3.1 (49% improvement), and in Liestal,  the MAE dropped from 8.49 to 8.47 (very slight improvement). These results may suggest that linear modeling that accounts for chill and warming could yield improvements over Quetelet’s model, whilst maintaining the simplicity of the original design. However, it is important to acknowledge that cherry bloom phenology is complex, making it difficult to entirely capture with linear models. As continued studies explore more nuanced approaches, they may highlight other factors that could improve this linear model, effectively revealing the deeper impacts of a changing climate.
